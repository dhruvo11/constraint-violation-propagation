## 5.3 BLENDED EDITS AND WHAT EACH BRANCH CATCHES

Table 5.3 and Figure 5.2 show the problem that motivated the project. From crude to blended edits the detection AUC of the visual branch drops on every type, and at its validation threshold it flags only 13.4% to 67.1% of blended edits, against 85.8% to 100% of crude ones. The rules do not notice any difference between the two: their AUC on blended edits is within 0.002 of their AUC on crude edits, and they flag every blended edit, because the signature depends only on the values. CVP keeps this property and flags 97.6% to 100% of blended edits while flagging only 1.2% to 5.8% of genuine documents.

The combination has a cost on the other side. Complete forgeries (T4) break no rule, so CVP can only catch them through the visual branch. It flags 38.2% to 65.5% of them, and its AUC on T4 (0.755 to 0.854) is lower than that of the visual branch alone (0.788 to 0.935), because the noisy-OR mixes the visual evidence with the constraint branch's score, which cannot tell a coherent edit from a genuine document. The learned constraint branch also absorbs OCR noise well: on the receipt, the bare rules flag 10.6% of genuine documents because of misread digits, while CVP flags only 1.2%.

## 5.4 THE AMBIGUITY SPLIT AND THE HYPOTHESES

On the ambiguity split, where the broken rules fit more than one field, the rules alone are at chance or below it on every type (20.0% on the transcript at a chance level of 20.0%, and 41.3% to 50.6% on the others at 44.6% to 50.0%), while they are right 94.2% to 100% of the time on unambiguous edits. CVP beats both single-modality baselines on this split for five of the six types, reaching 87.6% to 93.2%. The exception is the shop receipt (76.6% against 86.4% for the visual branch): editing the cash or the change breaks the same single rule, and the learned class probability leans towards one of the two fields and can override the heatmap. A variant with a shared prior, which spreads the probability evenly over the two fields, gets 90.9% of these edits right, but it was slightly worse on average on the validation data and so was not chosen. Table 5.4 summarises the four hypotheses.

!table Table 5.4: The Four Hypotheses and Their Outcome
!widths 8,34,20,38
Hyp. | Statement | Outcome | Evidence
H1 | The visual branch degrades from crude to blended edits; the rules do not | Supported on all six types | Visual AUC 0.972–0.999 falls to 0.788–0.933; rule AUC changes by at most 0.002
H2 | The rules alone are at chance on the ambiguity split | Supported on all six types | 20.0–50.6% against chance levels of 20.0–50.0%; 94.2–100% on unambiguous edits
H3 | CVP beats both single modalities on the ambiguity split, by a larger margin than overall | Partly supported | Best on 5 of 6 types; margin larger than overall on 3 types, equal on 1, smaller on 1
H4 | Candidate pooling beats concatenation fusion, mainly in attribution | Supported on all six types | 6.9–33.6 points better attribution; detection AUC within 0.021
!end

Concatenation fusion has access to the same evidence as CVP, yet its attribution is much worse. A network that sees a global summary of both branches learns that a document is forged far more easily than where it was forged; pooling the heatmap inside the fields suggested by the rules is what turns visual evidence into an attribution.

## 5.5 HOW MUCH VISUAL EVIDENCE IS NEEDED, AND ROBUSTNESS

Figure 5.3 changes what the visual branch saw during training. Trained on crude edits only (zero-shot), it gets almost no blended edits right (0.0% to 6.6%), yet CVP still attributes 62.1% to 92.3% of them, close to the rules alone (60.7% to 91.7%): wherever the broken rules point to a single field, the rules do the work. CVP is better than the rules alone on four types but worse on the transcript and the invoice, where the learned head trusts a heatmap that knows nothing about blended edits. With the older generator G1 (the main setting), the visual branch reaches 63.0% to 77.9% and CVP 83.0% to 95.5%, above both single modalities on every type. With the test generator itself (oracle), both exceed 97% on five types and the gap closes. CVP helps most when the visual evidence is weak but not completely missing.

## 5.6 CHOOSING THE ATTRIBUTION HEAD

Six designs of the attribution head and CVP (rule) were compared on a separate validation set of blended edits from the older generator, with the rule for choosing fixed beforehand: the best mean attribution over the six types (Table 5.5). The per-class prior with heat used as probabilities won with 97.0%, against 91.1% for CVP (rule), and it is the CVP reported throughout. On the test set, the designs trade blended single-field edits against complete forgeries: the chosen design is best on blended edits (88.9% on average), the log-space designs are best on complete forgeries (up to 74.6%), and no design wins everywhere. ## 5.7 REAL RECEIPTS (CORD)

On the 992 CORD receipts to which at least one rule applies, each rule holds on 96.6% to 99.7% of genuine receipts (Table 5.6, Figure 5.6), and 6.7% of genuine receipts break some rule even when every printed word is read correctly. Taking the values only at the fields CVP reads gives 7.2%, and when docTR reads those fields the figure rises to 10.9%. The extra 3.7 points are OCR errors. ## 5.8 REAL FORGERIES MADE BY PEOPLE (FIND IT AGAIN!)

The Find it again! forgeries were made by people with ordinary image editors. We read every scan with docTR and checked four receipt rules: the total, the change, cash covering the total, and Malaysian rounding (an adjustment of at most 0.02). Of the 987 scans available, 646 genuine and 121 forged scans had at least one rule we could check. Some rule fails on 32.2% of the forged scans against 17.2% of the genuine ones, a difference that is statistically significant (two-proportion z ≈ 3.8). The change rule alone fails on 31.4% of forged scans against 10.5% of genuine ones (Figure 5.10). Real forgers, in other words, break receipt arithmetic far more often than genuine receipts do.

!figure report/final_report/figures/findit_rules.png | 4.8 | Figure 5.10: Share of Genuine and Forged Find it again! Scans on Which Each Receipt Rule Fails

The visual branch, on the other hand, did not transfer to these forgeries (Table 5.8). Its heat separates forged from genuine test scans at chance level (AUC 0.504 to 0.527), and its hottest pixel falls inside an edited region for only 2.9% to 8.6% of forged scans. On real human forgeries, all of CVP's evidence currently comes from the rules.

## 5.9 RECEIPTS EDITED BY COMMERCIAL AI EDITORS (AIFORGE-DOC)

Our own forgeries of real photos still came from our own renderer. The real test is a forgery made by an actual AI image editor. AIForge-Doc (Wu et al., 2026a) makes forgeries the way a fraudster with such an editor would. For each document it picks one field, crops it with some context, asks a commercial AI editor to write a new value in the same style, and pastes only the field's box back into the photo. Amounts are multiplied by a random factor between 1.15 and 3 or between 0.2 and 0.85, and nothing else on the document is changed. Version 1 used Gemini 2.5 Flash Image and Ideogram v2 Edit; version 2 repeats exactly the same edits with GPT-Image-2. On version 2, people comparing the forged and the authentic image side by side were at chance (0.501), and the best published detectors reached only 0.599 (TruFor) and 0.585 (DocTamper) AUC (Wu et al., 2026b).

In each version, 983 forgeries were made from CORD receipts, one per receipt (in version 1, Gemini made 885 of them and Ideogram 98). Because CORD labels every printed value, we ran CVP on these receipts exactly as in Section 5.7, with the same OCR, rules, visual branches and thresholds, and nothing was trained or tuned on AIForge-Doc. Each forgery is pixel-identical to its CORD photo outside the edited box (we checked all 983), so the authentic half of each pair is the CORD photo itself, and the two images differ only where the editor wrote.

These edits are the kind the rules were written for. 84.6% of them change a value that some rule reads (the rest change item names and codes, or amounts that no rule on that receipt reads), and with the true values 95.4% of those break a rule, because the totals are never updated. The editors also write what they are asked to write: docTR reads the requested value back for 91.0% of the GPT-Image-2 edits, 74.5% of the Gemini edits and 50.0% of the Ideogram edits. As read by docTR, 82.9% (GPT-Image-2) and 82.2% (Gemini and Ideogram) of the edited receipts break a rule, against 12.2% of the authentic ones.

Table 5.9 and Figure 5.11 give the results. None of our visual branches can tell an AI-edited receipt from its authentic photo. The zero-shot branch puts almost no heat on these receipts at all (AUC 0.499 for GPT-Image-2 and 0.523 for Gemini and Ideogram), and the two branches fine-tuned on CORD photos reach only 0.502 to 0.560 on the 99 test receipts. The rules alone detect the edits with 0.846 and 0.843 AUC (0.907 and 0.904 when the edited field is one that a rule reads), with 95% bootstrap intervals of about ±0.015. CVP keeps this detection, because a broken rule outranks any heat, and with the zero-shot branch it raises an alarm on exactly the receipts the rules flag: 82.9% and 82.2% of the AI edits against 12.2% of the authentic receipts.

CVP also names the edited field more often than the rules alone (scored on the 832 edits to a field that a rule reads): 66.8% against 64.1% for GPT-Image-2, a gain whose 95% interval is 0.4 to 4.9 points, and 74.1% against 64.5% for Gemini and Ideogram (7.1 to 12.0 points). On the 471 receipts whose true pattern of broken rules fits more than one field, the rules alone are at chance (42.2% and 43.9%, with chance at 41.9%) and CVP reaches 46.9% and 60.9%. In other words, a heatmap that cannot tell a forged receipt from a genuine one can still be a little warmer on the edited field than on the other candidates, which is exactly the situation CVP was designed for. That signal is much weaker for GPT-Image-2 than for the older editors. With the fine-tuned branches, CVP names the edited field for 71.4% to 76.8% of the edits on the test receipts, against 61.7% to 63.1% for the rules, although with only 99 receipts the intervals are about ±10 points.

Figure 5.12 shows one receipt. GPT-Image-2 changed the subtotal from 194,000 to 109,293. The zero-shot heatmap is completely empty (its peak is 0.000), but the new value breaks both the sum rule and the total rule, and only the subtotal fits that pattern (orange box), so CVP names it (red box), which is correct (green box).

!figure report/final_report/figures/aiforge_example.png | 4.8 | Figure 5.12: A CORD Receipt Whose Subtotal GPT-Image-2 Changed From 194,000 to 109,293, With CVP's Evidence (AIForge-Doc v2, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)

The fine-tuned branches turned out to be sensitive to how many times an image has been saved as JPEG. Every document in our own CORD test set, genuine ones included, was saved once more at quality 90 (Section 5.7), and the AIForge-Doc images were not. Without that extra save, the branch fine-tuned on crude edits sees heat on the authentic photos (a median peak of 0.49, against 0.00 on the same photos in our own test set), so at its validation threshold CVP raises an alarm on 32.3% of authentic receipts instead of 14.3% (20.2% instead of 17.3% for the branch that saw every category). When both images of every test pair are saved once more at quality 90, the false alarms go back to 14.1% and 17.2%. CVP then flags 79.8% to 81.8% of the GPT-Image-2 edits and 76.8% to 78.8% of the Gemini and Ideogram edits, and it names the edited field for 75.6% to 76.8% of them with the fine-tuned branches, against 62.3% to 63.7% for the rules. The visual branches stay at chance (AUC 0.497 to 0.579). They miss these edits because of what the editors do, not because of the file format.

The published numbers cover all four source datasets of AIForge-Doc and get most of their signal from WildReceipt and SROIE, so they are not directly comparable with ours. What the comparison does show is where the evidence lies. Against these editors the pixels carry almost nothing, for our visual branches, for the published detectors and for people alike, while the arithmetic carries almost all of it. The editors are caught because they change one value and leave the rest of the receipt alone. A forger who also updated the totals (our T3 and T4 forgeries) would get past the rules, and none of our visual branches would see the edit.
