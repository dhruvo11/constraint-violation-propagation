# CHAPTER 6: INFERENCES

## 6.1 KEY TECHNICAL FINDINGS

The experiments support a small number of clear conclusions:

- **The rules do not care how good the forgery looks.** Blended edits that the visual branch alone flags only 13% to 67% of the time are caught 97.6% to 100% of the time, because they still break the arithmetic. This held on all six synthetic types and on real receipt photos.
- **The rules shortlist, the pixels choose.** Wherever the broken rules fit more than one field, rule checking alone is at chance, exactly as Proposition 1 predicts. Adding even weak visual evidence inside the shortlist lifts attribution well above chance (87.6% to 93.2% on five synthetic types, and 56.2% against 37–40% for the single modalities on real receipts with a zero-shot visual branch).
- **CVP helps most when the visual evidence is weak but present.** The gain over the visual branch is large when the visual branch has seen an older generator or none at all, and it shrinks to a few points when the visual branch has seen the test generator itself.
- **Where the heatmap is pooled matters more than what model fuses it.** A network that concatenates both branches globally detects forgeries almost as well as CVP but attributes 6.9 to 33.6 points worse.
- **Real receipts follow the rules, and real forgers break them.** The six receipt rules hold on 96.6% to 99.7% of genuine CORD receipts, and real human forgeries break a rule almost twice as often as genuine receipts (32.2% against 17.2%).
- **The limits are clear and measurable.** Coherent forgeries that fix every dependent field are only caught by the visual branch; a learned head can trust a weak heatmap too much; and OCR errors set a floor of about 7% to 17% false alarms on real receipts.

## 6.3 ALGORITHMIC INFERENCES

Three lessons from this project are useful beyond document forgery.

First, it is worth asking what a reasoning component can and cannot know before training anything. Proposition 1 told us in advance that rule checking alone must fail on certain edits, which decided the role of the visual branch and gave us a test (the ambiguity split) on which the design had to prove itself. The experiments then matched the theory closely.

Second, the consistent set is better than a single best guess. On real receipts, keeping every class that fits the broken rules (the consistent set) attributed 2.9 to 7.8 points better than keeping only the single most likely class, because the intervals of different classes overlap (the cash and the change), and a single choice throws away the true field whenever it picks the other class.

Third, a learned component has to know when to trust its inputs. The head that is best on average sometimes trusts a heatmap that has stopped being informative, for example after heavy downscaling or when the visual branch has never seen blended edits. Making that trust depend on how reliable the heatmap is, rather than fixing it once on clean data, is the most direct way to make the system more robust.
